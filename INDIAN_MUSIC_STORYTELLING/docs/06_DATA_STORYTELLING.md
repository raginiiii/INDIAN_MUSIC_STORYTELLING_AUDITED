# 06 — Data Storytelling

## Editorial approach
The archive should reveal relationships through narrative questions rather than display a dashboard of unrelated charts.

## Core storytelling questions
- How does place shape the materials and forms available to makers?
- What stages transform raw materials into an instrument?
- Which components create, transmit or modify sound?
- What knowledge does a maker contribute that cannot be reduced to a parts list?
- How do musicians adapt instruments through technique and performance?
- How are instruments connected across traditions, materials or mechanisms?
- What is changing in contemporary practice, and whose perspective describes that change?

## Candidate interactive narratives
1. **Made by place:** region-to-instrument exploration, with multi-location and uncertainty support.
2. **Inside the instrument:** annotated anatomy with selectable components.
3. **From material to sound:** craft-process sequence linked to materials and construction.
4. **Hands that make:** maker profiles connected to workshops, instruments and documented techniques.
5. **In the hands of musicians:** musician profiles and performance contexts.
6. **A family of forms:** related-instrument comparisons based on explicit relationship types.
7. **Hear the difference:** user-controlled recordings with contextual metadata.

## Data visualisation rules
- Use a map only when geographic evidence is sufficient and the map adds understanding.
- Do not encode certainty through precise pins when the evidence is broad or contested.
- Provide text alternatives for all diagrams and maps.
- Label illustrative diagrams as illustrative.
- Distinguish measured audio analysis from editorial waveform artwork.
- Never infer a sound property from an image or instrument category alone.

## Progressive disclosure
First layer: concise explanation and strong image.
Second layer: components, materials, process or people.
Third layer: detailed terminology, source notes, alternate interpretations and research caveats.

## Avoid
- decorative charts without a research question;
- ungrounded network diagrams;
- ranking traditions or instruments;
- false precision in dates, locations or material identification;
- presenting a single narrative as the only valid interpretation.
